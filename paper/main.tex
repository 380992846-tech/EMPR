\title{\textbf{Do Open LLMs Compute or Retrieve?\\
A Multi-Probe Study of Physics Reasoning in Open-Weight Language Models}}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Abstract
\begin{abstract}
We study whether open-weight language models actually \emph{compute} when they
answer multi-step physics problems, or whether they pattern-match against
memorised solutions. Rather than reporting a single accuracy number, we combine
five probes on SciBench physics (\nbSciBench{} problems) with a
multiple-choice control (MMLU physics, \nbMMLU{} problems) and a second
free-response benchmark (OlympiadBench physics, \nbOlympiadAll{} problems,
\nbOlympiadScorable{} numerically gradable).
Our main findings are: (i) prompting matters more than scale --- greedy
chain-of-thought roughly doubles accuracy over answer-only for the Qwen2.5 family
(\ratioSevenB$\times$ at 7B) while a two-fold parameter increase buys only
\gapFourteenVsSevenCot{} points; (ii) the same 7B model answers \accMMLU\% of
multiple-choice physics items but only \accCotSevenB\% when it must produce the
number itself, which rules out missing knowledge as the explanation;
(iii) a semantics-preserving paraphrase flips \pctChangedSevenBWording\% of final
answers while leaving accuracy unchanged, i.e.\ the flips are symmetric error
jitter, and this holds for every model we tested;
(iv) restoring clean residual states recovers behaviour mainly from the
early-middle layers (peak at layer \peakLayer{} of \nbLayers), and splitting
output execution from semantic correctness shows that parseability is essentially
layer-invariant (\pctParseCorrupt\% even under corruption) whereas correctness
tracks restored depth --- recoverable only for problems the model can already
solve;
(v) prompt-level scaffolding does not substitute for capability
(\scafDeltaPP{} points, McNemar $p=\scafP$).
An error-type annotation cross-checked by a second, cross-family judge shows that
the dominant failure is arithmetic rather than physical, but also that the error
taxonomy itself is annotator-dependent ($\kappa=\kappaAnnotators$), which we
report as a measurement limitation rather than hide.
All experiments were run on a single 80\,GB GPU in about \gpuHoursTotal{}
GPU-hours; code, per-run metadata and the dual-annotated sample are released.
\end{abstract}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 1 Intro
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Chain-of-thought prompting \citep{wei2022cot} is routinely credited with giving
language models ``reasoning'' ability, and physics benchmarks such as SciBench
\citep{wang2023scibench} and OlympiadBench \citep{he2024olympiadbench} are
routinely used to demonstrate progress. Yet a single accuracy number cannot
distinguish two very different internal situations: a model that derives the
answer from the stated quantities, and a model that recognises the problem
family and emits a plausible-looking number. The distinction matters for anyone
who wants to trust, audit or improve such systems.

This paper takes the distinction seriously and treats it as an empirical question
with several independent probes, rather than as a claim about model internals.
We ask three questions and answer them with measurements that are cheap,
deterministic and reproducible on a single GPU:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What does performance look like?} (\S\ref{sec:main}) Across five
        open-weight models on \nbSciBench{} SciBench physics problems, with
        bootstrap confidence intervals and per-sub-domain breakdowns.
  \item \textbf{Is the model computing or retrieving?} (\S\ref{sec:sensitivity})
        We perturb the same problems in three ways that are semantically
        different but superficially similar: change the numbers, rewrite the
        wording, and exchange two physical quantities.
  \item \textbf{If it computes, where?} (\S\ref{sec:causal}) We use
        corrupt-and-restore causal tracing to localise the computation in depth,
        and separate ``producing a number'' from ``producing the right number''.
\end{itemize}

Two further probes close the loop. A multiple-choice control (\S\ref{sec:cross})
holds knowledge fixed and varies only the answer format; an error-type analysis
with a cross-family annotator check (\S\ref{sec:errors}) asks \emph{which} stage
fails and how reliable such attributions are. We also test whether prompt-level
scaffolding can repair the instability we find (\S\ref{sec:scaffold}).

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A multi-probe protocol that separates computation from retrieval using
        only black-box generation plus (for one open-weight model) residual-stream
        interventions, together with the finding that \emph{paraphrase
        instability} is a reproducible, model-generic signal of
        surface-conditioned answering.
  \item A representation-vs-output decomposition: output execution is
        layer-invariant while correctness is layer-localised, and failures on
        problems the model cannot solve are \emph{not} recoverable by restoring
        any single layer.
  \item An honest measurement result: with a same-family and a cross-family judge
        annotating the same \nbAnnotated{} errors, agreement is only
        $\kappa=\kappaAnnotators$. We therefore claim only annotator-robust
        statements and release the dual-annotated sample as a resource for
        LLM-as-a-judge robustness research.
\end{enumerate}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 2 Related
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Physics and science reasoning benchmarks.}
SciBench \citep{wang2023scibench} collects college-level textbook problems with
free-response answers; we use its physics subsets and restrict to the
\nbSciBench{} problems whose reference answers are numerically gradable.
OlympiadBench \citep{he2024olympiadbench} contains competition problems; only
\pctOlympiadScorable\% of its physics items have numerically gradable references,
a coverage limit we carry through all our tables. MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}
provides multiple-choice knowledge questions and serves here as a format control
rather than as a target.

\paragraph{Causal tracing and localisation.}
Activation- and state-level interventions are standard for localising behaviour
in open models \citep{meng2022locating,stolfo2023mechanistic}. We use the
simplest variant --- corrupt the input, then restore the clean residual stream at
a single layer --- because it requires no training and yields a per-layer curve.
We deliberately restrict this probe to one open-weight model, since hidden-state
access is impossible for API-only models, and we report the strict inclusion rule
that shrinks the analysable sample.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and robustness of LLM answers.}
Prompt sensitivity is well documented \citep{sclar2023quantifying}, usually as a
nuisance to be averaged out. We instead treat sensitivity as the measurement: a
perturbation that leaves physics and data untouched but changes the answer is
direct evidence that the answer depends on surface form.

\paragraph{LLM-as-a-judge and error taxonomies.}
Using a strong model to label errors is common practice
\citep{zheng2023judging}, and its reliability is usually reported as agreement
with human labels. We contribute a case where two \emph{LLM} judges disagree
substantially on a subjective taxonomy, and argue that this disagreement is
itself the informative quantity.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 3 Method
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Benchmarks and grading}
All correctness decisions are made by symbolic/numeric comparison. The final
answer is extracted from the generation by a precedence-ordered procedure
(\texttt{ANSWER:} line, taking the last occurrence; then \texttt{\textbackslash
boxed\{\}}; then ``answer is $X$''; then the last number on the last line),
normalised for Unicode minus signs, superscripts and thousands separators, and
compared to the reference under a 5\% relative tolerance (0.01 absolute for
near-zero targets).

Reference answers are parsed with a \emph{stricter} parser than model outputs: a
reference that is not a single number is marked unscorable and removed from the
denominator rather than counted as a model error, because a permissive parser
reads expressions such as $x^2+2x$ as $2$ and silently corrupts the denominator.
For SciBench physics all \nbSciBench{} references are numerically gradable, so
the denominator is the full set; for OlympiadBench only
\nbOlympiadScorable{} of \nbOlympiadAll{} are, and we report that coverage
everywhere it applies. Grading is covered by \nbSelfCheck{} self-test cases and
\nbTests{} unit tests, and an end-to-end oracle check (feed the reference as the
model output) yields 100\% self-consistency.

\subsection{Models and inference}
We evaluate five open-weight instruct models: Qwen2.5-3B/7B/14B-Instruct
\citep{qwen2024}, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3}, and
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B \citep{guo2025deepseekr1}. Decoding is greedy with a
fixed seed, so every reported number is deterministic. We use the tokenizer's
chat template, left padding for batched generation, and the \texttt{transformers}
backend throughout; vLLM was unavailable in our environment, so no result mixes
backends.

\subsection{Perturbation probes}
Each probe is a deterministic transformation of the problem text:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{numeric}: scale physical values (physics intact, at most two
        numbers changed, exponents and power bases protected);
  \item \textbf{wording}: paraphrase the framing only, leaving physics and data
        bit-identical. This is the only variant whose reference answer remains
        valid, so it is the only one for which we report a paired accuracy
        comparison;
  \item \textbf{swap}: exchange two distinct physical quantities, producing a
        physically inconsistent problem.
\end{itemize}
Because the correct answer to a \emph{numeric} or \emph{swap} variant is not
directly computable, those variants are scored by the distribution of output
change, and we report median and p90 rather than the mean (the sensitivity score
$|\Delta|/(|base|+\epsilon)$ is dominated by outliers when the base answer is near
zero).

\subsection{Causal tracing}
The numeric quantities of a question are replaced by a semantically empty
placeholder; the corrupted prompt is run; then the clean residual stream is
restored at exactly one layer at a time, all other layers remaining corrupted.
Restoration is applied during prefill only and never to decode steps, and clean
and corrupted sequences are aligned from the right because they tokenise to
different lengths. Only problems whose clean run was correct \emph{and} whose
corrupted run lost score enter the recovery rate, which we report together with
the skip counts.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{How large is the effect of prompting versus scale?}
\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports SciBench physics accuracy for all five models under
both prompting conditions, with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals and
per-sub-domain breakdowns.

\input{table1_main.tex}

Prompting dominates scale. For the Qwen2.5 family, chain-of-thought multiplies
accuracy by \ratioThreeB$\times$, \ratioSevenB$\times$ and \ratioFourteenB$\times$
at 3B, 7B and 14B, whereas doubling parameters from 7B to 14B adds only
\gapFourteenVsSevenCot{} points (from \accCotSevenB\% to \accCotFourteenB\%).
The benefit of chain-of-thought is moreover family-dependent rather than a law of
nature: for Llama-3.1-8B the same comparison gives only
\ratioLlamaEightB$\times$ (\accCotLlamaEightB\% vs \accDirLlamaEightB\%). Family
effects can exceed scale effects --- the 3B Qwen model
(\accCotThreeB\%) outperforms the 8B Llama model
(\accCotLlamaEightB\%) --- which is why we report a spread of models rather than a
single scaling curve. The reasoning-distilled model
(DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, \accCotROneSevenB\%) does not beat its
same-size instruct sibling, which we attribute to the friction between long
reasoning traces and this benchmark's terse numeric answer format.

\subsection{Is the model computing or retrieving?}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reports the three perturbation probes for four models.

\input{table2_sensitivity.tex}

Changing the numbers moves the answer most of the time
(\pctChangedSevenBNumeric\% of answers change for Qwen2.5-7B), so the input values
do participate in the computation: the model is not a lookup table. However, a
paraphrase that leaves physics and data bit-identical changes
\pctChangedSevenBWording\% of answers with no measurable accuracy effect, and the
pattern repeats across models (\pctChangedThreeBWording\%,
\pctChangedFourteenBWording\%, \pctChangedLlamaEightBWording\%). Paired McNemar
tests on the paraphrase variant are non-significant for every model
($p=\pairPSevenB$, $p=\pairPFourteenB$, $p=\pairPThreeB$, $p=\pairPLlamaEightB$),
so the flips are symmetric error jitter rather than a difficulty increase. The
magnitude of the response to \emph{numeric} changes also tracks accuracy within
the Qwen family (\pctChangedThreeBNumeric\% $\rightarrow$
\pctChangedSevenBNumeric\% $\rightarrow$ \pctChangedFourteenBNumeric\%), with
Llama-3.1-8B the least responsive (\pctChangedLlamaEightBNumeric\%) and the least
accurate. Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} visualises this contrast.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

           (\emph{numeric}) versus when only the phrasing is rewritten

\end{figure}

\subsection{If it computes, where --- and is the output on the same pathway?}
\label{sec:causal}
Table~\ref{tab:causal} locates the computation in depth.
Of \nbCausalAttempts{} attempted problems, \nbCausalValid{} satisfy the strict
inclusion rule (\nbCausalCleanWrong{} skipped as clean-wrong, \nbCausalFlat{} as
corruption-flat). Recovery peaks at layer \peakLayer{} of \nbLayers{}
($+\peakRecovery$), plateaus over the early-middle layers and collapses after
layer 18 (Figure~\ref{fig:causal}).

\input{table3_causal.tex}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.72\linewidth]{causal_tracing_Qwen2.5-7B.png}
  \caption{Recovery rate as a function of the restored layer, with $\pm1$ standard
           deviation across problems. Behaviour is recoverable mainly from the
           early-middle layers.}
  \label{fig:causal}
\end{figure}

Restoring a clean state at a layer is only defined for problems whose clean run is
correct, so this curve cannot tell us whether layer patching could \emph{rescue}
failures. To separate the two questions we instrument all
\nbAllAttempts{} attempted problems and record two outcomes at every restore
point: whether the model emits a parseable number (output execution) and whether
that number is correct (semantics). Table~\ref{tab:layer-parse-acc} shows the
result, split by whether the clean run was correct.

\input{table6_layer_parse_vs_acc.tex}

Parseability is essentially layer-invariant: it is \pctParseCorrupt\% even for the
corrupted baseline and stays within \pctParseMin--\pctParseMax\% at every restore
point. The model always emits a number; corruption never makes it decline.
Correctness, by contrast, depends on the restored depth: for the
\nbCleanCorrect{} problems the clean run solves, restoration recovers up to
\pctAccCleanCorrectBest\% of them, whereas for the \nbCleanWrong{} problems it
cannot, never exceeding \pctAccCleanWrongBest\% at any single layer. The two
pathways are therefore distinct --- and, importantly, the failures are capability
limits rather than misplaced representations that a layer patch could repair.

\subsection{Knowledge or execution? A format control}
\label{sec:cross}
Table~\ref{tab:cross-task} holds the model and the subject domain fixed and varies
only the answer format. The same Qwen2.5-7B gets \accMMLU\% of \nbMMLU{}
multiple-choice physics items right but only \accCotSevenB\% when it must produce
the number itself. Since the multiple-choice items test the same physics, missing
knowledge cannot be the explanation: the knowledge is retrievable, the numerical
execution is not.

\input{table5_cross_task.tex}

OlympiadBench provides a second free-response benchmark: accuracy rises from
\accOlySevenB\% to \accOlyFourteenB\% with scale (+ \gapOlyFourteenVsSeven{}
points), consistent in direction with SciBench, but its
\pctOlympiadScorable\% gradable coverage is biased towards simpler items and is
therefore never compared numerically against SciBench.

\subsection{Which stage fails, and can we tell reliably?}
\label{sec:errors}
We annotated every SciBench chain-of-thought error with a three-way scheme ---
E1 wrong physical law, E2 algebraic/arithmetic/unit error, E3 violated constraint
--- and repeated the annotation with a second, cross-family judge on the same
samples.

\input{table4_errors.tex}

Under both judges E2 dominates and E3 stays small, and the 7B/14B distributions do
not differ significantly, i.e.\ the larger model gets more problems right rather
than failing differently. This matches the format control above: the bottleneck is
execution, not physical knowledge.

Agreement between the two judges is, however, only
$\kappa=\kappaAnnotators$ (\pctAgreeAnnotators\% raw agreement over
\nbAnnotated{} samples). The precise E1/E2 split is therefore \emph{annotator
dependent}: applying the wrong law usually also produces wrong arithmetic, so the
observable text is consistent with either label. We report the coarse,
annotator-robust statement only, and treat the disagreement as a finding about
error taxonomies rather than as an inconvenience (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Can prompt scaffolding repair the instability?}
\label{sec:scaffold}
If the paraphrase instability were caused by a missing solution scaffold, telling
the model to write down the knowns and unknowns explicitly should help. At a
matched token budget on the same \nbScafCot{} problems, it changes accuracy from
\accScafCot\% to \accScafStruct\% (\scafDeltaPP{} points, McNemar
$p=\scafP$) --- no reliable gain (Table~\ref{tab:scaffold}). Prompt-level
scaffolding does not substitute for capability.

\input{table7_prompt_scaffold.tex}

The same table exposes a separate measurement limitation worth flagging: between
\pctNoAnswerCot\% and \pctNoAnswerStruct\% of outputs ignore the explicitly
requested \texttt{ANSWER:} line and are graded through the fallback extractor.
Output lengths stay far below the token budget, so this is a format-compliance
failure, not truncation, and accuracy is statistically indistinguishable between
compliant and non-compliant outputs.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
All numbers come from a single GPU class and a \texttt{transformers} backend without
continuous batching, so wall-clock times are not comparable to a vLLM deployment,
although greedy decoding makes the accuracies backend-independent. Causal tracing
covers one open-weight model and the strict-inclusion subset
(\nbCausalValid{} of \nbCausalAttempts{}), so layer localisation is a qualitative
early-middle pattern rather than a precise per-layer estimate. OlympiadBench
numbers refer to the \pctOlympiadScorable\% of problems with numerically gradable
references. Error-type shares are annotator-dependent
($\kappa=\kappaAnnotators$) and are therefore reported coarsely. The paraphrase
probe uses deterministic rule-based rewriting; a model-generated paraphrase would
be stronger but introduces rewrite quality as a confound. Finally, prompt-level
scaffolding was tested with one prompt design at one budget, so its null result is
evidence against, not proof of impossibility.
